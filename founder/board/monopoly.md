# Monopoly lens
Score: 4 | Vote: FUND IF

## What is strong
- **A small market that can be named and counted.** 150 schools in Hyderabad and Secunderabad, split into four zones with one founder each. The Monopoly lens favours exactly this: start with a large share of a small market. Owning one zone is a goal you can measure.
- **The route to customers is the school, not ads to parents.** One school decision can bring in a whole cohort of students. That is a real answer to the lens's question of how the first 100 customers arrive, if pilots convert.
- **A product that works and is already built.** It has diagnostics, adaptive flows, TTS narration and parent visibility, so the team is testing distribution, not still building.
- **Possible data moat.** Diagnostic and mastery data per student, per school, mapped to local syllabi, could build up into know-how a newcomer would not have. Today it is only possible. Nothing yet shows the data exists or that it improves the product.

## What would kill it (the top 3, most dangerous first)
1. **No proprietary edge.** Adaptive practice and AI explanations built on OpenAI APIs can be copied in months. Large national K-12 edtech platforms, school ERP/LMS vendors adding AI features, and free general-purpose AI chat tools all reach the same school and parent. Nothing yet stops a copycat in month six, and nothing is shown to be 10x better on something that matters.
2. **Distribution that never compounds.** School sales are slow and done face to face, with demos, proposals, pilots, orientation and onboarding. If every school costs weeks of founder time and Hyderabad does not produce references that pull in the next school, there are no economies of scale. The business becomes a services operation.
3. **Margin squeezed from both sides.** At ₹299 per paid user, the school revenue share and the per-student OpenAI cost both come out of the same small amount. Neither number is known. The lens says competition destroys profit, and a commodity AI layer sold through intermediaries is where that happens.

## The questions I would ask the founder
- What is the secret? What do you know about Hyderabad schools, or how these students learn, that the national players do not?
- Which one thing is 10x better than what a school uses today? How would you measure it?
- Which zone will you own first, and what share of its schools counts as "owning" it?
- Who else is already selling to these 150 schools, and why did those schools buy them?
- What does it cost to acquire one school, counted in founder hours and cash, and how long from first meeting to signed pilot?
- What is the AI API cost per active student per month? What is the school revenue share?
- Does student data make the product better for the next student, in a way a competitor cannot match without the same data?
- Why now? What has changed that makes this possible today and not three years ago?

## If FUND IF: the conditions
1. **Own one zone first.** Signed paid or structured pilots with a set share of one zone's schools (the founders propose the target; we suggest at least 25% of that zone) within an agreed window.
2. **Proof it is clearly better.** A measured learning gain between baseline and follow-up diagnostics in pilot cohorts, compared with a non-using group, plus weekly active use above an agreed threshold after 8 weeks.
3. **Unit economics on paper with real inputs.** Net revenue per paid user after the school share, minus AI and hosting cost per user, is positive and documented from pilot data.
4. **Distribution that repeats.** At least one new school signed through a referral from an existing pilot school, not through cold outreach.
5. **A written answer to "what stops a copycat in month six"** that rests on something the team owns (data, syllabus mapping, school relationships), not on the OpenAI model.
